Learning **how to make papercraft** is one of the most rewarding paper crafts you can pick up. Unlike origami, which uses only folding, papercraft combines cutting, scoring, folding, and gluing to build three-dimensional models from flat printed sheets. With a few basic tools and a free printable template, you can create animals, characters, vehicles, and buildings right on your desk.

## What You Need to Get Started

Before you make your first cut, gather these essentials:

- **Cardstock** — 160–220 gsm is ideal for most models. Heavier cardstock (220–250 gsm) works better for large structural pieces like dragons and buildings.
- **Sharp scissors or a craft knife** — precision scissors work for curves; a craft knife with a fresh blade is better for straight cuts and fine details.
- **Cutting mat** — protects your table and gives you a grid for measuring.
- **Ruler** — a steel ruler is best for cutting straight lines; a clear plastic ruler works for measuring.
- **Bone folder** — creates clean, sharp creases. If you don't have one, the back of a spoon works in a pinch.
- **Glue** — PVA glue for strong structural bonds, or a glue stick for quick tabs.

You'll also need a standard inkjet or laser printer. Print at 100% scale — do not select "fit to page" or the tabs won't line up.

## Step-by-Step: How to Assemble a Papercraft Model

### 1. Print the Template

Print your template on cardstock at 100% scale. Let the ink dry for a few minutes before handling — smudged ink shows on the finished model. If you're using inkjet paper, avoid touching the printed surface with sweaty fingers.

### 2. Cut Out the Pieces

Cut along the **solid lines** only. Dashed lines are fold lines — do not cut them. Cut out the largest pieces first, then the smaller ones. For intricate areas, make small, controlled cuts rather than trying to cut a curve in one motion.

**Pro tip:** Cut slightly outside the line if you're a beginner — you can always trim more, but you can't add paper back.

### 3. Score the Fold Lines

Use your bone folder (or the back of a spoon) to score every dashed line. Scoring creates a shallow groove that makes folding clean and precise. Score on the **back** (unprinted side) of the paper for mountain folds, or on the front for valley folds.

This is the step most beginners skip — and it's the difference between a model that looks professional and one that looks messy.

### 4. Fold Each Tab

Fold every tab inward (toward the unprinted side). Use your bone folder to make a sharp crease. Tabs are the glue flaps that hold the model together — if they're not folded cleanly, the seams will be visible.

### 5. Glue and Assemble

Apply a thin, even layer of glue to each tab. Too much glue warps the paper and shows through the printed side. Press the tab firmly against the matching piece and hold for 10 seconds.

**Assemble from the largest piece outward.** Start with the main body or base, then attach smaller parts. Let each major section dry for a few minutes before adding the next — trying to build everything at once leads to misaligned parts.

## Common Beginner Mistakes (and How to Avoid Them)

1. **Skipping the scoring step** — scored folds are clean; unscored folds are rounded and weak.
2. **Using too much glue** — a thin layer is enough. Excess glue warps cardstock and leaves visible stains.
3. **Printing at "fit to page"** — always print at 100% scale or the tabs won't match.
4. **Cutting dashed lines** — solid lines = cuts, dashed lines = folds. Cutting a fold line weakens the model.
5. **Rushing the glue stage** — let each section dry before moving on. Patience gives cleaner results.

## Your First Project

For your first papercraft, choose a simple model with fewer than 10 pieces. Animals like cats, foxes, and birds are great beginner projects — they have simple geometry and stand easily. Once you've built 2–3 simple models, you'll develop the muscle memory for clean cuts and sharp folds. From there, you can tackle more complex models like dragons, castles, and detailed characters.

Papercraft is a skill that improves with every model you build. The first one might not be perfect — but the tenth one will surprise you.
