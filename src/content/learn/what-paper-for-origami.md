If you have ever asked **what paper to use for origami**, you are already thinking like a folder who cares about results. The honest answer is "it depends on the model." A sheet that makes a perfect simple flower will fight you on a dense dragon, and vice versa. This guide maps paper to project type so you can grab the right square every time.

## Start with the standard square

Before special cases, know the baseline: most origami is folded from thin, square sheets around 70–90 gsm. That thinness is the point. Thin paper lets you stack many layers at a wing tip or a neck without the model ballooning open. The global standard size is 15 cm (about 6 inches); if you are unsure, start there.

## Match paper to project type

### Simple folds and first models

For your earliest projects — a [origami flower](/origami/flower/), a boat, a basic crane — use standard kami. It is light, colorful, and cheap enough to waste on practice. The creases read clearly, which is exactly what a beginner needs.

### Complex and layered models

When a design piles up ten or more layers (many animals and 3D shapes), switch to tant or a slightly heavier washi. These hold a sharp crease under pressure and do not spring back the way flimsy paper does. A [origami frog](/origami/frog/) that needs a springy jump benefits from a sheet with a little body.

### Wet folding for soft curves

Wet folding — damping the sheet so it stays where you shape it — demands strong, absorbent paper. Traditional washi or a quality tissue-backed paper works; standard kami will disintegrate if it gets too wet. Use this technique for organic, sculptural pieces rather than geometric ones.

### Kids and classroom crafts

With children, durability beats finesse. Slightly heavier paper (or even trimmed printer paper for very young kids) survives eager hands and repeated unfolding. Bright colors keep attention. Save delicate washi for quiet display work, not the craft table.

## Building a small paper stash

You do not need a huge inventory. A practical starter shelf looks like this:

- One pack of 15 cm kami (assorted colors) for daily folding.
- A smaller pack of tant for gift-quality models.
- A few sheets of foil for curves and experiments.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Using cardstock for detailed models.** It is too thick; layers will not close.
- **Folding damp or wrinkled paper.** Creases come out uneven and the model looks tired.
- **Ignoring the two-tone effect.** Many designs rely on a white back showing through; plain single-color paper hides that.
