You do not need a craft shop to start folding. Learning **how to make origami paper** at home lets you match colors to a project, rescue a sheet that is the wrong size, and build two-tone paper that makes reverse folds easier to read. This guide covers three skills — cutting a true square, dyeing plain paper, and making your own two-tone sheet — using supplies you likely already have. It pairs well with our [origami paper size](/learn/origami-paper-size/) reference and our take on [using printer paper for origami](/learn/printer-paper-for-origami/).

## Why make your own

Shop paper is convenient, but homemade sheets give you control. You choose the exact color, the exact size, and whether the back is white or a second color. That matters for models where the reverse side shows, like a [paper crane](/origami/crane/) with visible inner flaps. Homemade paper is also a good way to use up scraps and practice without wasting a premium pack.

## Skill 1 — Cutting a true square

Most origami fails start with a rectangle that was not actually square. Here is a reliable method that does not need a ruler:

1. Start with a rectangular sheet (printer paper works).
2. Fold one short edge down to meet the long edge, forming a triangle; crease and unfold.
3. Cut off the strip below that diagonal fold.
4. You now have a perfect square; fold it corner to corner to confirm both diagonals match.

This "fold-and-trim" trick guarantees a square even if your scissors are slightly off. For standard practice, aim for a 15 cm square, or follow the charts in our [origami paper size](/learn/origami-paper-size/) guide to trim from A4.

## Skill 2 — Dyeing and coloring plain paper

Once you have a square, you can give it color with whatever you have:

- **Markers or watercolor:** lay down even bands or a soft wash; let it dry fully before folding so creases stay sharp.
- **Coffee or tea stain:** brushes a warm tone onto white paper for a vintage look — great for [paper craft templates](/templates/) styled as aged maps.
- **Wax crayon rubbings:** press paper over a textured surface and rub to transfer a pattern.

Keep the coating light. Heavy paint makes the sheet stiff and cracks along folds. Thin, even color is the goal.

## Skill 3 — Building two-tone paper

A two-tone sheet (colored on one side, white or a second color on the back) is the secret behind readable reverse folds. To make one:

1. Take two thin squares of different colors.
2. Brush a thin layer of glue stick onto the back of one.
3. Press the other square on top, colored side out, and smooth out air bubbles.
4. Let it dry flat under a book so it stays even.

The result behaves like kami: you get a clear "front" and "back" that makes complex steps far easier to follow, especially on a [gift box](/origami/box/) where inside and outside colors both show.

## Tips for cleaner homemade paper

- **Press, don't rush.** Let glued sheets dry under weight; a curled sheet folds unevenly.
- **Match weights.** Glue two similar-weight papers so the double layer creases as one.
- **Test a fold.** Before committing a whole pack, fold a small test square to check it holds a crease.
- **Store flat.** Stack finished sheets between board or inside a folder.

## When homemade paper is enough — and when it is not

Homemade paper is perfect for practice, custom colors, and kids' projects. For display-quality models, though, machine-made kami and tant give more uniform results.
