Nothing is more frustrating than spending an hour building a beautiful papercraft model, only to watch it tip over the second you let go. If your papercraft won't stand up, the problem is almost always one of three things: the base is too narrow, the center of gravity is too high, or the internal structure is too weak.

Here are 7 proven techniques to make any papercraft model stand on its own.

## 1. Widen the Base

The single most effective fix is to give your model a wider footprint. A base that's 2–3 times wider than the model's height creates a stable platform.

- **For animals:** Make the feet larger or add a base plate under all four feet.
- **For characters:** Widen the stance — feet shoulder-width apart is more stable than feet together.
- **For buildings:** The base is usually already wide; focus on even weight distribution.

If the template doesn't include a wide base, you can add one yourself: cut a circle or rectangle from extra cardstock and glue the model's feet to it.

## 2. Add Weight to the Base

A heavy bottom keeps the center of gravity low, which makes the model harder to tip over.

**Options for weighting:**
- Glue a few coins (pennies work well) to the inside of the base before closing it up.
- Use a small metal washer or a piece of scrap metal.
- For larger models, glue a flat stone or a heavy bead inside.

The weight should be as low as possible — ideally at the very bottom of the model. Even 10–15 grams of extra weight at the base makes a noticeable difference.

## 3. Use Internal Supports

Many papercraft models are hollow inside, which makes them flexible and prone to collapsing. Adding internal supports stiffens the structure.

**Simple internal supports:**
- **Cardstock ribs** — cut thin strips of cardstock and glue them horizontally inside the body, like ribs. This prevents the body from squishing.
- **Diagonal braces** — glue a strip of cardstock diagonally across the inside of large flat panels to prevent bowing.
- **Cardboard core** — for very large models, glue a small cardboard box or tube inside as a structural core.

You don't need to fill the entire interior — a few well-placed supports at stress points (joints, the base, the neck) are enough.

## 4. Get the Center of Gravity Right

The center of gravity (CoG) is the point where the model's weight is concentrated. For a model to stand, the CoG must be directly above the base.

**Common CoG problems:**
- **Head too heavy** — characters with big heads (chibi style, cartoon figures) tend to tip forward. Add weight to the feet or base to counterbalance.
- **Arms outstretched** — arms extended to the sides shift the CoG outward. Either pose the arms closer to the body or add a wider base.
- **Tail heavy** — dragons and animals with long tails tip backward. Glue a small weight inside the tail tip, or counterbalance with weight in the chest.

**Rule of thumb:** If you can balance the model on one finger at its midpoint, the CoG is probably fine. If it tips, add weight to the opposite side.

## 5. Use Heavier Cardstock for Structural Parts

Thin paper (80–120 gsm) is too floppy for standing models. Use 180–220 gsm cardstock for the main body and base — it's stiff enough to hold its shape without being too thick to fold.

- **Base and legs:** 220–250 gsm (heaviest)
- **Main body:** 180–220 gsm
- **Small details and accessories:** 160–180 gsm

If you've already printed on thin paper, you can glue the printed sheet to a heavier cardstock backing before cutting — this adds stiffness without reprinting.

## 6. Glue Joints Properly

Weak glue joints are a hidden cause of tipping. If a leg or base panel comes loose even slightly, the model loses stability.

**Best practices for strong joints:**
- Use PVA glue for all structural joints (legs, base, body panels).
- Apply glue to the entire tab surface, not just the edges.
- Press firmly for 10–15 seconds per joint.
- Let each major joint dry for 5 minutes before moving on.
- Reinforce critical joints (leg-to-body, base-to-body) with an extra dot of glue on the inside.

## 7. Use a Display Stand

When all else fails — or when you want a clean, professional display — use a stand.

**Stand options:**
- **Clear acrylic stand** — invisible support, great for display. Available in various sizes online.
- **Papercraft base** — cut a decorative base from cardstock and glue the model to it. Add a label with the model's name.
- **Fishing line / invisible thread** — for flying models (dragons, birds, planes), hang them from the ceiling or a stand with clear thread.
- **Wire armature** — for very complex models, insert a thin wire inside before closing it up. The wire provides structural support and lets you pose the model.

## Quick Troubleshooting

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Tips forward | Add weight to base/feet; widen base |
| Tips backward | Weight the chest; shorten/lighten tail |
| Legs buckle | Heavier cardstock; internal leg supports |
| Body squishes | Add internal ribs/cardboard core |
| Won't stay on base | Wider base; glue feet firmly |
| Floppy overall | Switch to 200+ gsm cardstock |

Most standing problems can be solved with a wider base and a few grams of weight inside. Start with those two, then add internal supports if needed. Browse our [papercraft gallery](/gallery/) for models with stable bases — many are designed with standing in mind.
